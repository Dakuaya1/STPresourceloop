WHY DO IT

Three reasons.
One system.

  • Under India’s Solid Waste Management Rules 2016 ↗, a society generating 50 kg or more of waste a day is a bulk waste generator. It must segregate its waste and process the wet portion on site.
  • 1,000 families make about 675 kg of food waste a day, more than 13 times that threshold.
  • Many societies are not yet compliant. In Mumbai, 1,401 of 2,825 bulk generators had yet to process their own wet waste (Free Press Journal ↗).
  • Nearby restaurants and hotels face the same rule, so they need somewhere to send their wet waste.
  • Methane captured, not released. Food waste rotting in dumps gives off methane, a greenhouse gas far stronger than carbon dioxide. In a sealed digester it is captured and burned for energy.
  • Cleaner power. Electricity from biogas replaces grid power, most of which in India still comes from coal.
  • Fewer trucks. Sludge and wet waste are treated where they are produced instead of being hauled across the city.
  • Nutrients back to the soil. Tested compost goes to gardens and landscaping in place of chemical fertiliser.

Model assumptions & cost boundaries +
  • Every estimate includes all three feeds: STP sludge, segregated food waste and green waste (leaves, garden and landscape waste). 4.5 people per home (editable above); 100 homes per acre for green-waste scaling.
  • 100 litres of sewage per person per day; STP capacity is population × 100 L/day, converted to MLD.
  • Sludge biogas: 9 L/person/day theoretical yield × 55% capture efficiency. Food waste: 0.15 kg/person/day × 0.12 m³ biogas/kg.
  • Gas output basis: “Field-proven” multiplies design biogas yields by 0.45, which matches about 0.09 kWh of net electricity per kg of food waste reported by Pune plants (Ashok Meadows, PMC Katraj) and BARC’s figure of about 60 m³ of biogas per tonne. “Design yield” uses the full workbook yields.
  • Energy: 6 kWh per m³ biogas × 32% generator electrical efficiency. The plant’s own electricity use is deducted: 10% manual, 12% hybrid, 15% automated.
  • Digester sizing: sludge at 0.035 kg dry solids/person/day, thickened to 5% solids, plus food waste at about 1 litre per kg. Digester volume = daily feed × retention time × 1.15 for headspace. A covered digestate tank holds 10 more days of feed and is priced at half the digester rate. The workbook core cost already includes 50 m³ of digester per MLD; only the volume above that is added.
  • Digester tank cost: ₹15,000 per m³ central (low ×0.6, high ×1.4). This is a placeholder for an installed tank with mixing and insulation; replace it with vendor rates.
  • Core capex: ₹40 / ₹55 / ₹70 lakh per MLD for low / central / high, scaled by max(0.55, (MLD ÷ 1.5)−0.25).
  • Improvements: thickening to 8% solids uses the thickener already in the core scope. Outside organic waste is digested like residents’ food waste. Compost output: solids left after digestion (about 55% of what goes in, food waste at 25% solids) at 35% moisture, plus green-waste compost at 35% yield.
  • Food-waste preparation (sorting table, shredder, slurry tank, screen, chopper pump): ₹3,000 per kg/day capacity up to 1 tonne/day, getting cheaper per kg above that (× tonnes−0.15). Composting equipment: ₹2,000 per kg/day of garden waste (5 kg/acre/day). ±30% for low/high.
  • Automation: manual none; hybrid ₹6 lakh + 8% of equipment (sensors, dosing pumps, controls, alerts, screw press); automated ₹15 lakh + 20% (controller, automatic valves for tank rotation, pasteuriser, automatic dewatering, in-vessel composter). Planning placeholders; confirm with vendor quotations.
  • Commissioning and seeding: seed material for 20% of digester volume at ₹1,000 per m³ (cow dung or digested sludge, including transport), plus 3% of capital cost for start-up support, testing and operator training. Allow 1–2 months of reduced output while the digester builds up.
  • Running costs: extra operator time ₹12,000 / ₹6,000 / ₹3,000 per month (manual / hybrid / automated) up to 1 tonne/day of organic waste, growing with tonnage (× tonnes0.8), on top of the existing STP operator; maintenance and spares 2% / 2.5% / 3.5% per year of equipment cost (core, food and compost equipment, automation) plus 0.5% of digester tank cost; lab testing of compost batches and process ₹50,000 per year; consumables such as gas-cleaning media and pH buffer at ₹1 per m³ of biogas.
  • Not included: financing, taxes, permits, land, civil variations and major equipment replacement. The range is a scale-based estimate, not a design-specific quotation.

The developer case

THE DEVELOPER CASE

Turn a running cost
into a lasting resource.

An STP earns nothing back. Sludge has to be treated and transported, month after month, and organic waste leaves the site the same way.

The proposal is to add an engineered digestion and gas-use system to the STP the project already has, so that sludge and organic waste become feed instead of a disposal bill. For a new project, the additional cost can be considered during utility planning and overall development budgeting. For an occupied community, the decision rests on measured waste supply, space and the operating case.

The value comes from usable energy and managed outputs. The feasibility study needs to establish how much of that value remains after collection, operation, maintenance and the recovery system’s own electricity demand.

01 / AVOIDED COST

Stop paying to haul sludge away

Digest sludge on site to cut what the community spends today on sludge treatment and transport.

02 / MORE FEED, MORE GAS

Use every organic stream

Food waste, leaves and garden waste go into the digester or the composter, which raises the output and keeps the waste on site.

03 / ENERGY

Offset purchased power

Use cleaned biogas in a correctly sized generator, with performance measured at the usable output.

04 / FOR THE LIFE OF THE BUILDING

A feed that never stops

As long as people live there, they produce sewage and kitchen waste every day. The system’s fuel is built in.

How it runs

HOW IT RUNS

Sorted at home.
Fed in minutes.

Residents separate food waste in their own kitchens, and housekeeping staff already collect it every day. So the plant starts at the shredder.

  1. 01

    At home

    Residents keep food waste separate from dry waste and rejects in their own kitchens. Clean segregation is the most important input to the whole system.

    Residents
  2. 02

    Collection

    Housekeeping staff pick up segregated wet waste on their existing daily round. Garden staff bring leaves and clippings.

    Housekeeping
  3. 03

    Weigh & check

    Each day’s bins are weighed and logged. Any plastic, shells or bones that slipped through are picked out.

    Operator
  4. 04

    Shred & feed

    Food waste goes through the shredder, mixes with thickened STP sludge into a slurry and is pumped to the digester.

    Operator or automatic
  5. 05

    Digest

    The slurry stays 25–30 days in the sealed digester. A covered second tank collects the gas that remains.

    The process
  6. 06

    Energy

    Biogas is cleaned and runs a generator for common services such as STP pumps, lighting and lifts.

    Automatic
  7. 07

    Compost & test

    Digestate is dewatered, composted with leaves in dated batches and released only after each batch passes its tests.

    Operator + lab

Questions

PRACTICAL QUESTIONS

Before you commit.

Is a housing society required to process its wet waste?

Yes, if it generates 50 kg or more of waste a day. Under the Solid Waste Management Rules 2016, such a society is a bulk waste generator and must segregate its waste and process the wet portion on site, as compost or biogas. A society of a few hundred families crosses that threshold easily. Check your municipality’s current notification for local deadlines and penalties.

Where does the saving come from?

Mainly two places. First, sludge that is treated and transported off site today is digested on site, which cuts that recurring bill. Second, the biogas produced, mostly from food waste, runs a generator that offsets purchased electricity.

Has this been done anywhere?

Yes. Ashok Meadows in Pune has run a food-waste biogas plant for 550 homes since 2017, and Pune Municipal Corporation runs decentralised biogas plants for streetlights. In Europe, Hamburg, Helsingborg, Sneek and Ghent digest household sewage with food or green waste in neighbourhood plants, and East Bay MUD in the USA adds food waste to its sewage digesters. See “Proven elsewhere” for details and sources.

Why add food waste, leaves and garden waste?

STP sludge alone gives a modest amount of gas. Segregated food waste is a much richer feed and raises biogas output several times over, while leaves and garden waste can be composted in the same loop. Residents produce this waste every day for as long as they live there, so the feed lasts the life of the building.

Who sorts and feeds the waste?

Residents separate food waste at home, and housekeeping staff collect it on their existing daily round. At the plant, a staff member weighs it, removes any remaining plastic and loads the shredder. In the hybrid and automated options, everything after the shredder runs on its own.

Does this replace the STP?

No. The STP continues treating sewage. The proposed biodigester processes suitable sludge from that system, with food waste added where feasible. Returned liquids and their treatment load must be included in the design.

Is the space already available?

The project already has sewage infrastructure and may have utility space that can be used. A survey must confirm room for the digester, gas equipment, waste handling, access and digestate processing. Additional space or civil works may be necessary.

How much electricity can it produce?

Output depends on occupied homes, actual sludge and food-waste quantities, methane yield and operating uptime. A feasibility study should establish net usable electricity after the system’s own consumption. No fixed generation or payback is promised at concept stage.

Can the remaining material go straight to gardens?

Not automatically. Digestate requires appropriate treatment and testing before use. The design must account for both liquids and solids, compost curing where needed, and the suitability of the finished product.

Who operates the system after handover?

A trained operator and maintenance arrangement are essential. The handover plan should assign waste collection, equipment upkeep, gas safety, output monitoring and operating costs to clearly identified parties.

What is needed to assess a project?

The STP process and capacity, actual occupancy and flows, sludge characteristics, segregated food-waste quantities, a utility-area layout, electricity bills and current waste-management costs. These form the starting point for a site-specific proposal.
